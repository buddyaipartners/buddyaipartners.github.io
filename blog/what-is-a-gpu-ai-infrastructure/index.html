What is a GPU and Why Is It the Engine of Modern AI? The Jensen Huang & NVIDIA Paradigm Explained

How Parallel Computing, Tensor Cores, and Blackwell Architecture Transformed Artificial Intelligence from Science Fiction to Global Infrastructure.

By Rahul Kumar Dhal · Google Certified AI Architect • November 16, 2026 • 9 min read

Artificial Intelligence is not code running on general-purpose computers. AI is massive mathematical transformation running across thousands of micro-cores working in unison. At the heart of this revolution is the GPU (Graphics Processing Unit) — and the twenty-year vision of NVIDIA Founder and CEO Jensen Huang.

1. CPU vs. GPU: Sequential Precision vs. Massive Parallelism

A modern CPU (Central Processing Unit) from Intel or AMD has 8 to 64 high-speed cores designed to execute complex, linear tasks one after another — like a brilliant professor solving an intricate calculus problem step-by-step.

A GPU (Graphics Processing Unit), by contrast, has 10,000 to 18,000 smaller cores designed to perform simple arithmetic calculations simultaneously — like a stadium of 15,000 students doing basic multiplication at the exact same second.

When GPUs were invented, their purpose was 3D computer graphics: calculating the exact color, lighting, and vector coordinates of 8 million pixels 60 to 120 times every second. But in the late 2000s, computer scientists realized something profound: the linear algebra used to compute 3D geometry is identical to the matrix multiplication required to train deep neural networks.

2. The 2006 CUDA Gamble That Built a $3.5 Trillion Empire

In 2006, Jensen Huang made an audacious bet that Wall Street heavily penalized: he invested billions of dollars to build CUDA (Compute Unified Device Architecture). CUDA turned every graphics card NVIDIA sold into a general-purpose scientific supercomputer, allowing programmers to write C++ and Python directly for the GPU dies.

In 2012, Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton trained "AlexNet" on two consumer GeForce GTX 580 gaming GPUs, crushing traditional CPU computer vision benchmarks. That historic milestone ignited the modern deep learning era.

3. Inside Tensor Cores, Precision Math, and HBM3e Memory

Modern enterprise AI accelerators — like the Hopper H100, H200, and Blackwell B200 — are no longer graphics cards. They are purpose-built AI engines containing specialized silicon:

  • Tensor Cores: Hardware units that compute 4x4 matrix multiplications in a single clock cycle using reduced-precision arithmetic (FP8, FP16, and INT8), delivering petaflops of compute while slashing power draw.
  • High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM3e): Large Language Models (LLMs) are memory-bandwidth bound. Modern GPUs stack memory chips vertically right on the interposer, streaming over 8 Terabytes per second directly to the compute cores.
  • NVLink Interconnect: High-speed optical/copper interconnects connecting hundreds of GPUs so they act as a single, giant, unified compute chip.

4. Training vs. Inference: What Indian Founders Must Understand

AI Training: Teaching a foundational model from scratch (like Llama 3 or Gemini) requires thousands of interconnected GPUs running continuously for months. This is done by hyperscalers spending hundreds of millions of dollars.

AI Inference: Running the trained model to answer an incoming customer query, draft an email, or qualify a property buyer in sub-800 milliseconds. Today, optimized inference runtimes (like vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, and Ollama) allow businesses to run quantized, fine-tuned 8B to 70B models on modest dedicated GPUs or on-device NPUs with total privacy and zero SaaS per-token fees.
